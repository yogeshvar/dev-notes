## The Study: A Hands-On Approach to XR

The foundation of our exploration lies in a workshop set at ETH Zurich, where graduate students in civil engineering and architecture interacted with various XR devices to complete assembly and disassembly tasks. The workshop featured a unique setup known as the "AdapTable," specifically designed for this purpose. Participants were guided through disassembling and reassembling this table under different conditions, all while using XR technologies.

To gauge user experience, the study utilized two well-established metrics: the NASA Task Load Index (TLX), which measures cognitive demand, and the System Usability Scale (SUS), assessing how user-friendly each device is. Participants were required to download specific applications, like Fologram, beforehand to ensure smooth integration with the XR environments.

### Understanding XR Technologies

Before delving into the findings, it’s essential to understand the three primary categories of XR technologies that were tested:

1. **Augmented Reality (AR)**: This overlays digital information on the real world via devices such as smartphones and tablets, allowing users to see a digital guide right in their physical workspace. Think of it like a GPS navigation system—but for assembling furniture instead of finding directions.

2. **Mixed Reality (MR)**: MR takes things a step further by blending the real and digital worlds in a way that allows interactive experiences. Users can manipulate digital elements and see them affected by their physical environment.

3. **Virtual Reality (VR)**: This immerses users in a fully digital environment, cutting off the physical world entirely. While VR can create outstanding simulations, it can be isolating, which might not be ideal for collaborative tasks like assembly.

## Key Findings: What the Students Learned

The results from the workshop were revealing and provided valuable insights into the usability and cognitive load experienced while using these XR devices:

- **Usability Scores**: Augmented Reality emerged as the star performer, scoring the highest in usability. It was evident that participants found AR to be intuitive and engaging, making it easier to follow along with complex assembly instructions.

- **Cognitive Demand**: Interestingly, while MR devices scored slightly lower in usability, they excelled in cognitive demand, indicating that they provided a less taxing user experience. This balance could make MR a valuable option when fatigue and focus are concerns during lengthy tasks.

- **Increased Engagement**: Participants left the workshop with a newfound interest in XR technologies, recognizing their potential to enhance circular construction practices and increase efficiency in the AEC sector.

## Real-World Implications: Why This Matters

The findings from this study advocate for the systematic evaluation of XR technologies before their implementation in real-world projects. With many professionals still wary of incorporating new technologies into their workflows, the insights gained from hands-on experience with XR devices can guide informed decision-making.

To contextualize these findings, imagine an architect and a construction crew using AR glasses that overlay step-by-step disassembly instructions onto a deconstructed table. This allows for precise tasks to be executed with minimal supervision, drastically reducing errors and enhancing productivity. On the other hand, an MR headset could provide a collaborative environment where team members around the world can visualize complex structures together, interacting with them in real-time.
